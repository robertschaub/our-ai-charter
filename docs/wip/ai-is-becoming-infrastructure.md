# AI Is Becoming Infrastructure. It Must Remain Answerable to People.

*The essential argument of Our AI Charter—from sovereignty and public infrastructure to runtime governance, empathy, and remedy.*

AI is becoming part of the infrastructure through which people learn, work, communicate, govern, and make sense of the world. The decisive question is therefore no longer only which model performs best. It is whether people and democratic institutions can **inspect, shape, contest, and switch** the systems on which they increasingly depend—and whether those systems remain answerable when their use affects a life, a right, or a public decision.

That question connects the articles and public posts behind Our AI Charter™. They began from different concerns: fragile dependence on a few providers, the limits of national AI strategies, factual claims that cannot be checked, automated actions that outrun their authority, and systems that sound caring while making people less able to correct or resist them. They converge on one proposition:

> **Free societies need a federated Public AI network: open and plural enough to resist unilateral control, governed by shared public obligations, and backed by evidence, independent review, and remedy.**

This is not only a technology proposal. It is a proposal about power.

## Sovereignty is the capacity to act—not the illusion of standing alone

Digital dependence is real. A provider can change access, prices, rules, or system behaviour. Supply chains and compute can become instruments of pressure. Public institutions may come to rely on systems they cannot inspect or replace on acceptable terms.

The understandable response is to call for national models, national clouds, and national control. Domestic capability matters. But [autarky is not sovereignty](../Published/swiss-autarky-illusion.md).

Autarky is the ambition to manage without others. Sovereignty is the capacity to act despite interdependence: to help set the rules, inspect systems independently, contest consequential decisions, and switch providers without collapse. A country that owns one model but lacks alternatives, shared standards, skilled people, reliable partners, and an effective route to challenge decisions may own an artefact without being sovereign over the system around it.

Sovereignty is also not an end in itself. Resilience preserves room to act; values determine whom that room serves. The relevant tests are human dignity and freedom, self-determination and privacy, truth and verifiability, justice and the rule of law, and reciprocal reliability. Institutional control is not yet public accountability: neither a foreign vendor nor a domestic institution should hold the rules, the evidence, and the appeal path at once.

The wider aim is international: free societies should be able to cooperate without surrendering local control or accepting domination by a single state or company. That was the central claim of the early articles on [AI sovereignty and resilience](../Published/ai-sovereignty-and-resilience.md) and [the Public AI Network](../Published/the-public-ai-network.md).

## Open models are necessary—and not enough

Open, inspectable models widen access, support research and adaptation, and reduce dependence on systems that cannot be examined. But open weights do not tell a public body which service actually processed its data, whether a model changed, how a disputed output was produced, or who will answer when an AI-supported decision harms someone.

A collection of national models is therefore not yet public infrastructure. The current [Our AI Charter project statement](../Published/our-ai-charter-public-ai-infrastructure.md) joins four foundations:

1. **Open, plural models.** People and institutions should be able to inspect and compare models across languages, jurisdictions, capabilities, and known limits. Network discovery and rules should not give one provider unilateral power to shape access.
2. **Data and provenance commons.** Origin, permissions, opt-outs, restrictions, and access conditions should be documented. Rights and provenance work should be reusable instead of imposed afresh on every public-interest team.
3. **Shared assurance and evaluation.** Claims about systems and their behaviour should be backed by scoped, checkable evidence, with correction, independent review, and remedy where the evidence or outcome fails.
4. **Federated public AI infrastructure.** Independently operated compute nodes should cooperate under shared rules, preserving local control while providing interoperability and fallback paths.

The four foundations depend on distributed competence and anti-capture governance. Infrastructure can be purchased; the ability to understand, govern, and improve it must be built. A registry captured by one operator can shape the market. An evidence process controlled by the system it assesses can manufacture trust. A federation without enforceable obligations can reproduce the dependency it was meant to overcome.

Here, **public** does not describe one legal form or require one central operator. It describes the obligations, access, accountability, and governance of the infrastructure. Public, academic, cooperative, nonprofit, and mission-aligned commercial nodes could contribute under the same rules—if those rules remain transparent, contestable, and protected from capture. Participation should depend on verifiable commitments to human rights, rule-of-law procedures, democratic accountability, transparency, and effective remedy—not on a state or institution simply describing itself as free.

The proposal is intended as a contribution to the wider public-AI movement, not a claim of affiliation with any existing coalition.

## Start with the people who bear the consequences

Infrastructure and governance can sound remote until a decision lands on someone. The [practical test for power](../Published/a-practical-test-for-power.md) starts there: follow a use of power to the people who bear its cost.

For any AI-supported decision, ask:

- Who has power over whom, what makes its use legitimate, and who benefits or bears the cost?
- Were those affected heard early enough to matter, and is there a less harmful path?
- Can they safely disagree, refuse, or challenge—and can an independent body stop, reverse, or investigate?
- How will harm be remedied, can repeated failure limit the power, and which boundaries hold even when they frustrate the goal?

These questions lead to the Charter's five public obligations: AI-supported systems and the institutions using them should be **purpose-bound; answerable to people; safe, secure, private, and resilient; fair in practice; and open to evidence and correction**. The launch article, [*Trustworthy AI, Accountable to People*](../Published/trustworthy-ai-accountable-to-people.md), framed the essential test plainly: can a material claim or consequential decision be traced, tested, challenged, and corrected—and is a named human institution responsible for what follows?

Broad principles matter, but they are not self-executing. They need to reach both the life of a system and the life of each action it helps take.

## Govern the transition where information becomes power

AI governance has two connected clocks.

The first is the system lifecycle:

**design → deploy → operate → incident → remedy**

The second is one AI-supported action:

**plan → prepare → check → decide → review**

The draft Charter action path turns those moments into five gates:

1. **Plan → Authorize.** Is AI—and this system—appropriate here? Who has authority, for what purpose, and within which limits?
2. **Prepare → Submit.** May these data, sources, instructions, and tool outputs enter the action path under the applicable rights, privacy, and confidentiality rules?
3. **Check → Verify.** Is the evidence sufficient for this use, with uncertainty, disagreement, and missing perspectives still visible?
4. **Decide → Commit.** May this exact proposal become an external action under current authority and policy?
5. **Review → Rely.** Does the decision remain supportable, monitored, correctable, challengeable, and reversible or withdrawable where possible?

The rule developed in [*When Should Runtime AI Governance Interrupt?*](../Published/when-should-runtime-ai-governance-interrupt.md) is to **govern every authority-bearing transition—not every inference or mechanical step**.

That avoids two opposite failures. Asking a person to approve every step produces ceremonial clicking. Asking nothing after deployment lets a live system move beyond what anyone assessed. Routine, reversible actions can proceed silently inside a bounded mandate. Stop or escalate when evidence becomes a consequential decision basis, protected data cross a trust boundary, authority expands, a proposal becomes an external effect, or the system leaves its approved conditions.

The acting model must not approve itself. A component outside it should return **allow, deny, or escalate** against current policy and authority; the service producing the external effect should verify that decision again. Missing, expired, revoked, broadened, or replayed authority should fail closed. A person may choose among valid options, but a click cannot create a legal, policy, evidentiary, or institutional basis that is absent.

Autonomy therefore needs a bounded operating envelope, not endless prompts or unlimited standing permission. Its purpose, tools, data, recipients, duration, limits, delegation, monitoring, rollback, and revocation should be explicit. Aggregate limits matter too: a thousand individually admissible actions can compose an effect nobody authorized.

And a green light is not enough. Each consequential action needs a scoped, tamper-evident record of its basis, authority, decision, effect, and challenge route. The record can bind to sensitive evidence kept under protected, role-scoped, logged access without copying raw personal data into a public plane. Its subject should be the action and its authority—not every token or a person's whole conversation. Accountability must not become surveillance.

## No one body should control the accountability chain

Runtime checking answers *when* a system should stop. It does not establish who wrote a fair rule, whether the evidence is good, whether a reviewer is independent, or whether an affected person can obtain a binding remedy.

The companion article on [*who gets to check*](../Published/when-vs-who-ai-governance.md) therefore separates five roles:

1. **Rulemaker** — publishes the criteria but does not operate or audit the system.
2. **Operator** — runs the gate against signed policy but does not hold the only record.
3. **Record keeper** — preserves sealed evidence but cannot inspect it or decide a case alone.
4. **Independent reviewer** — assesses the evidence under assignment and funding arrangements the operator cannot control.
5. **Remedy decider** — hears the affected person's challenge and, where empowered, can bind the outcome.

No one body gets the whole chain. Accreditation and peer review may eventually check the checkers; disclosed and diversified funding, outcome-independent fees, and assignment methods that operators do not control can reduce capture. But role diagrams and cryptography cannot create legitimate authority. Where no law, tribunal, contract, or other enforceable arrangement can authorize access and bind a remedy, the claim must stay open about that limit.

The same honesty should govern assurance itself. A provider's “trustworthy AI” label is a declaration. A deterministic gate can prove that declared rules were applied to recorded inputs; it cannot prove that the rules were legitimate, the evidence true, the outcome fair, or the remedy effective. Any assurance claim must be scoped to the system, version, use, jurisdiction, and questions actually assessed.

## Empathy means correctable perspective—not artificial feeling

Rules and records can still miss what power does to a person. That is why the Charter's later articles add [dialogue, correction, and human answerability](../Published/empathy-is-a-practice.md).

AI does not need to claim human feeling. The defensible practice is disciplined perspective-taking: listen to affected people where possible, distinguish what they say from what the system infers, keep disagreement and uncertainty visible, and remain open to correction.

The operating loop is:

**engage → act → observe → disclose → correct → re-engage**

Dialogue should be safe, accessible, early enough to change an outcome, and connected to independent review and remedy. When direct dialogue is impossible, the institution should use the best available evidence and legitimate representation, disclose what remains inferred or unknown, choose the more cautious path, and preserve later challenge. A model must never fill in missing voices and declare them heard.

Empathy alone is not justice. It can be selective, favour the most emotionally vivid case, or become a tool for manipulation. **Sycophancy is not empathy. Personalization is not care.** Intelligence may find a path; empathy makes human consequences visible; justice sets the limits; legitimate authority determines who decides; and accountability keeps that power answerable.

For builders, [the practical rule](../Published/how-to-build-ai-that-acts-with-empathy.md) is:

> **Train for perspective-taking. Tune for truthfulness and agency. Adapt only with consent. Improve only through governed, testable releases.**

At runtime, keep four things distinct: what a person said; what the system inferred; what the person confirmed; and what the system has revocable permission to use or remember. An interpretation must never silently become fact. Feedback may improve the next response, but it must not silently become persistent memory, training data, or a model update.

The aim is not attachment or longer engagement. It is grounded, correctable behaviour that protects agency, makes misunderstanding easy to repair, and makes unwarranted trust difficult to acquire. The human or institution using AI remains answerable; the model cannot supply legitimate authority, independent review, or remedy.

## Make the public path simple—and the claims honest

The user experience should be simpler than the governance beneath it:

**find → check → use**

Find a system suited to the purpose. Check its jurisdiction, openness, evidence status, capabilities, and known limits. Use it through a service that preserves the relevant rules, records, and routes to challenge.

The first buildable substrate proposed by Our AI Charter is deliberately bounded: a model-plural policy broker connected to at least two independently operated compute nodes, plus an evidence plane. The broker would decide what may run, where, and under which purpose and data rules. The evidence plane would preserve the records needed for review, with public disclosure where safe and protected, logged access where sensitivity requires it. Raw personal data should not enter a public evidence plane.

This would not replace model builders, public-compute projects, standards bodies, or existing institutions. It would add the connective tissue between them: accountable routing, evidence duties, independent assessment, contestability, correction, and protection against capture.

But the maturity claim matters as much as the design. Our AI Charter is in **Phase 1: public drafting and connection-building**. It is not an operating network, live pilot, coalition, certification scheme, registry, assessor, or adjudicator. No model or provider is Charter-certified. What exists is a public body of work that others can inspect, criticise, improve, or reject.

The project's current roadmap uses Geneva 2027 as a deadline for a bounded Public AI Governance & Evidence Package: a governance blueprint, one evidence-and-evaluation pilot outline, a neutral clarification process, and a path toward a multi-stakeholder roundtable. The route is **alliance and mandate first, lawmaking later**. Switzerland is one possible host and bridge—not the owner of an international network.

## Built by many. Accountable to all.

The standard is more demanding than releasing model weights, buying a national system, adding a runtime gate, or issuing a trust label. Free societies need public capacity around open, plural models so that people and institutions can inspect, help govern, contest, and switch the systems they rely on. Every consequential use of AI-supported power should remain within exact authority, carry reviewable evidence, preserve dialogue and correction, and answer to the people who bear its consequences through independent review and effective remedy.

The next contribution should be concrete: identify an overclaim, show where the proposal duplicates good work, test one evidence duty, improve one safeguard, or help find a legitimate institutional home. The Charter is public because its claims should be contestable from the beginning.

**Built by many. Accountable to all.**
