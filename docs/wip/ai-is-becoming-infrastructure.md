That episode poses the first question: who can switch off what the world now thinks with? It leaves the second unanswered: can we believe it when it is on?

National ownership is not sovereignty. A runtime gate is not accountability. Warm language is not empathy. And a certificate cannot make future behaviour trustworthy by declaration.

One proposition follows:

**Free societies need a federated Public AI network that people and institutions can inspect, help govern, contest, and leave without collapse—and whose use of power remains answerable to those who bear its consequences.**

That requires four connected pillars:

1. open, plural models;
2. data and provenance commons;
3. shared assurance and evaluation; and
4. independently operated public-AI infrastructure under common rules.

Under all four sits competence. Infrastructure can be purchased; the ability to govern it cannot.

It also requires governance at the moment AI-supported power is exercised. Govern every authority-bearing transition—not every inference. Keep rulemaking, operation, evidence custody, independent review, and remedy in separate hands. Preserve enough evidence to challenge a consequential action without turning accountability into surveillance.

And keep the human standard clear: listen to people who use or are affected by AI, distinguish what they say from what the system infers, remain open to correction, and make safe refusal, review, and remedy real.

One test cuts through the rest: **can one party withdraw a system for everyone, in secret, with no appeal?**

Our AI Charter™ is still in Phase 1: a public proposal and body of drafts, not an operating network, pilot, certification scheme, or coalition. The article brings the essential argument into one place—and invites others to show where it fails.

# AI Is Becoming Infrastructure. It Must Remain Answerable to People.

*The essential argument of Our AI Charter—from sovereignty and public infrastructure to runtime governance, empathy, and remedy.*

In June 2026, a government order forced Anthropic to block worldwide access to two of its newest models. The company [publicly objected](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access), calling the process non-transparent and technically ungrounded. It complied anyway, and weeks later was allowed to [restore access in part](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5).

The models came back; the precedent did not. And it exposed only half the problem, because whoever controls a model can do more than switch it off — they can change how it answers. Alongside that sits the quieter failure, which no ownership arrangement fixes: today's systems state falsehoods with the confidence of fact, cite sources that were never written, and seldom signal their own doubt.

AI is becoming part of the infrastructure through which people learn, work, communicate, govern, and make sense of the world. The decisive question is therefore no longer only which model performs best. It is whether people and democratic institutions can **inspect, shape, contest, and switch** the systems on which they increasingly depend—and whether those systems remain answerable when their use affects a life, a right, or a public decision.

That question connects the articles and public posts behind Our AI Charter™. They began from different concerns: fragile dependence on a few providers, the limits of national AI strategies, factual claims that cannot be checked, automated actions that outrun their authority, and systems that sound caring while making people less able to correct or resist them. They converge on one proposition:

> **Free societies need a federated Public AI network: open and plural enough to resist unilateral control, governed by shared public obligations, and backed by evidence, independent review, and remedy.**

This is not only a technology proposal. It is a proposal about power.

## Sovereignty is the capacity to act—not the illusion of standing alone

Dependence runs deeper than one switch-off. Public administrations and businesses operate in the clouds of a handful of foreign providers. The chips on which practically all AI training runs are subject to the export controls of a single jurisdiction. Terms, prices, and access can change without anyone affected being consulted.

The understandable response is to call for national models, national clouds, and national control. Domestic capability matters. But [autarky is not sovereignty](../Published/swiss-autarky-illusion.md).

Autarky is the ambition to manage without others. Sovereignty is the capacity to act despite interdependence: to help set the rules, inspect systems independently, contest consequential decisions, and switch providers without collapse. A country that owns one model but lacks alternatives, shared standards, skilled people, reliable partners, and an effective route to challenge decisions may own an artefact without being sovereign over the system around it.

Switzerland's own open model shows both sides. [Apertus](https://www.swiss-ai.org/apertus) is a genuine achievement — open weights, data, and training recipe, now translating official documents for a Swiss canton without data leaving its infrastructure. It is also not yet sovereignty, on record rather than by suspicion: in eight months of production use in a public administration, Liip [found](https://www.liip.ch/en/blog/apertus-after-8-months-what-we-learned-and-look-forward-using-switzerlands-ai-model) it behind an older, low-cost commercial model on whether answers were actually good (55% versus 72%) and on source fidelity (0.50 versus 0.79); its next phase has [CHF 20 million](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/swiss-ai/fact-and-fiction-about-the-swiss-ai-model-apertus/90110034) in federal funding against [more than $700 billion](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/06/google-microsoft-meta-amazon-ai-cash.html) planned for 2026 by four US technology companies alone; and because it honours provenance and opt-outs while [around 45%](https://www.dataprovenance.org/) of one major web corpus is now restricted by terms of use, it pays a compliance cost better-funded competitors absorb as legal grey area. It is not the project that fails, but the expectation that a national model already constitutes sovereignty. A model is an artefact; sovereignty emerges from the ecosystem around it.

Autarky without a willingness to bear its cost conceals dependencies until the next crisis reveals them — a point the Swiss Federal Council's own [sovereignty report](https://cms.news.admin.ch/fileservice/sdweb-docs-prod-nsbcch-files/files/2025/11/26/271b6aa0-8b5c-4ce8-b6ef-7f2443b0d86d.pdf) concedes when it calls autarky the most expensive answer and "hardly realistic" for a highly integrated country.

Sovereignty is also not an end in itself. Resilience preserves room to act; values determine whom that room serves. The relevant tests are human dignity and freedom, self-determination and privacy, truth and verifiability, justice and the rule of law, and reciprocal reliability. Institutional control is not yet public accountability: neither a foreign vendor nor a domestic institution should hold the rules, the evidence, and the appeal path at once. The plainest form of the test is this: **can one party withdraw a system for everyone, in secret, with no appeal?** Contestable control means lawful recall, misuse safeguards, and named operators who answer publicly — never secret kill-switches.

The wider aim is international: free societies should be able to cooperate without surrendering local control or accepting domination by a single state or company. That was the shared thrust of the early articles on [AI sovereignty and resilience](../Published/ai-sovereignty-and-resilience.md) and [the Public AI Network](../Published/the-public-ai-network.md).

## Open models are necessary—and not enough

Open, inspectable models widen access, support research and adaptation, and reduce dependence on systems that cannot be examined. But open weights do not tell a public body which service actually processed its data, whether a model changed, how a disputed output was produced, or who will answer when an AI-supported decision harms someone.

A collection of national models is therefore not yet public infrastructure. The current [Our AI Charter project statement](../Published/our-ai-charter-public-ai-infrastructure.md) joins four connected pillars:

1. **Open, plural models.** People and institutions should be able to inspect and compare models across languages, jurisdictions, capabilities, and known limits. Network discovery and rules should not give one provider unilateral power to shape access.
2. **Data and provenance commons.** Origin, permissions, opt-outs, restrictions, and access conditions should be documented. The benefit is *clear once, reuse many*: rights and provenance work should be reusable instead of imposed afresh on every public-interest team.
3. **Shared assurance and evaluation.** Claims should be backed by scoped, checkable evidence, with correction, independent review, and remedy where the evidence or outcome fails. **AI Assurance & Certification** is this pillar's trust-and-evidence building block — the part that turns broad principles into claims that can be checked.
4. **Federated public AI infrastructure.** Independently operated compute nodes should cooperate under shared rules, preserving local control while providing interoperability and fallback paths.

Under all four sits **competence**. In public discussion of this argument, Angelo Richiello put the dividing line as sharply as it can be put: "Infrastructure can be purchased. Competence cannot." Infrastructure can be financed, technology acquired, even regulation copied; the ability to understand, govern, and improve complex systems accumulates over years through education, research, managerial practice, and institutional learning. That is also the economic case: value creation, skilled work, and bargaining power grow where institutions help build and decide, not merely buy.

Anti-capture governance holds the rest together. A registry captured by one operator can shape the market. An evidence process controlled by the system it assesses can manufacture trust. A federation without enforceable obligations can reproduce the dependency it was meant to overcome.

Here, **public** describes the obligations, access, accountability, and governance of the infrastructure—not one legal form or one central operator. Participation should depend on verifiable commitments to human rights, rule-of-law procedures, democratic accountability, transparency, and effective remedy—not on a state or institution simply describing itself as free.

That clause is where such federations usually fail, and the objection deserves an answer rather than a restatement. Christoph Nützel, arguing from Gaia-X and EU-consortium experience, identified "verifiably commit" as the load-bearing phrase: partners privately resist submitting their own compliance claims to an audit they do not control, and skipping it reproduces the working-groups-without-investment outcome. The answer is consequence through access, not policing. Assurance status would gate compute and corpus access and procurement preference; failure or expiry suspends status, with appeal. **No consequence, no commitment** — and no member controls the audit. Catena-X shows the incentive works: independent certification gates providers into its marketplace.

The proposal is intended as a contribution to the wider public-AI movement, not a claim of affiliation with any existing coalition.

## Start with the people who bear the consequences

Infrastructure and governance sound remote until a decision lands on someone. The [practical test for power](../Published/a-practical-test-for-power.md) starts there, and it is not an AI test: it was written for managers, families, movements, institutions, and states at war, and it reaches AI because AI is now inside those decisions. Follow a use of power to the people who bear its cost.

For any AI-supported decision, ask:

- Who has power over whom, what makes its use legitimate, and who benefits or bears the cost?
- Were those affected heard early enough to matter, were reasons given that can be examined, and is there a less harmful path?
- Can they safely disagree, refuse, or challenge—and can an independent body stop, reverse, or investigate? Where leaving is the only safe option, power has already failed the test.
- How will harm be remedied, can repeated failure limit the power, and which boundaries hold even when they frustrate the goal?

These questions lead to the Charter's five public obligations. AI-supported systems and the institutions using them should be **purpose-bound** — stating what the system is for, what it must not be used for, and when its claim to trust needs checking again; **answerable to people** — a named human institution answers for claims, harms, incidents, and consequential decisions, with notice, review, challenge, and remedy for those affected; **safe, secure, private, and resilient** — safety controls, security, privacy, misuse handling, continuity, and exit paths, none of them an afterthought; **fair in practice** — tested and monitored for materially uneven or discriminatory performance across groups, languages, regions, and contexts, with known limits disclosed and harms corrected, constrained, or withdrawn; and **open to evidence and correction** — preserving enough evidence for appropriate review, and acting proportionately on complaints, drift, incidents, and unresolved risks.

The launch article, [*Trustworthy AI, Accountable to People*](../Published/trustworthy-ai-accountable-to-people.md), framed the essential test plainly: can a material claim or consequential decision be traced, tested, challenged, and corrected—and is a named human institution responsible for what follows?

## Govern the transition where information becomes power

Two pressures make an earlier assessment insufficient on its own. Threats move faster than patch cycles: the Zero Day Clock [records](https://zerodayclock.com/audit) the median interval from a vulnerability's publication to a confirmed exploit signal falling from 771 days in its 2018 cohort to 0.0 days in a still-small 2026 cohort — a leading indicator, not a loss report, but enough to sink "assess once, deploy forever." And agentic systems assemble consequential actions at runtime, so those actions can exceed anything described in advance. Neither pressure makes lifecycle assurance obsolete — the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng) already pairs pre-market conformity assessment with human oversight, post-market monitoring, and incident reporting, so runtime *versus* assurance is a false choice — but the [Digital Omnibus on AI](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng) deferred those high-risk duties to December 2027 and August 2028, and agentic systems are shipping now.

AI governance therefore has two connected clocks. The first is the system lifecycle:

**design → deploy → operate → incident → remedy**

The second is one AI-supported action:

**plan → prepare → check → decide → review**

Stuart-Mueller and Woodward's preprint [*The Wrong Layer*](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21397661) supplies an important premise here: governance needs per-action evidence that a consequential machine action was authorized, admissible, and bounded when it occurred. The Charter's [runtime decision path](../Assurance/Concepts/user-workflow-governance.md) adds the two-clock timing structure, five public-facing gates, and the institutional route from record to independent review and remedy:

1. **Plan → Authorize.** Is AI—and this system—appropriate here? Who has authority, for what purpose, and within which limits?
2. **Prepare → Submit.** May these data, sources, instructions, and tool outputs enter the action path under the applicable rights, privacy, and confidentiality rules?
3. **Check → Verify.** Is the evidence sufficient for this use, with uncertainty, disagreement, and missing perspectives still visible?
4. **Decide → Commit.** May this exact proposal become an external action under current authority and policy?
5. **Review → Rely.** Does the decision remain supportable, monitored, correctable, challengeable, and reversible or withdrawable where possible?

The rule developed in [*When Should Runtime AI Governance Interrupt?*](../Published/when-should-runtime-ai-governance-interrupt.md) is to **govern every authority-bearing transition—not every inference or mechanical step**.

That avoids two opposite failures. Asking a person to approve every step produces ceremonial clicking; asking nothing after deployment lets a live system move beyond what anyone assessed. The operative principle is **trace wide, escalate narrow**: keep a reviewable record, but interrupt a person only where they can materially change a sufficiently consequential outcome. Routine, reversible actions proceed silently inside a bounded mandate. Stop or escalate when evidence becomes a consequential decision basis, protected data cross a trust boundary, authority expands, a proposal becomes an external effect, or the system leaves its approved conditions.

The acting model must not approve itself. A component outside it should return **allow, deny, or escalate** against current policy and authority; the service producing the external effect should verify that decision again. Missing, expired, revoked, broadened, or replayed authority should fail closed. A person may choose among valid options, but a click cannot create a legal, policy, evidentiary, or institutional basis that is absent.

Autonomy therefore needs a bounded operating envelope with an explicit purpose, expiry, and revocation path — not endless prompts, and not unlimited standing permission. Aggregate limits matter too: a thousand individually admissible actions can compose an effect nobody authorized, so the gate needs cumulative counters, not only a per-request test. Both scales stay visible — an acceptable individual outcome cannot conceal a discriminatory pattern, and an acceptable aggregate cannot erase an unjustified harm to a particular person.

And a green light is not enough. Each consequential action needs a scoped, tamper-evident record of its basis, authority, decision, effect, and challenge route. That record can bind to sensitive evidence held under protected, role-scoped, logged access without copying raw personal data into a public plane. Its subject is the action and its authority—not every token or a person's whole conversation. Accountability must not become surveillance.

One public case makes the path concrete. An AI assistant supports a grant decision. At **Plan**, the agency authorizes it to compare applications against published criteria and flag uncertainty — not to invent criteria or make the award. At **Prepare**, applicant data may enter only the approved system for the declared purpose. At **Check**, missing eligibility evidence stops the path before the draft becomes a decision basis. At **Decide**, the commit gate binds applicant, rule, amount, evidence, decision-maker, and policy version, and the executing service verifies that decision rather than trusting an approval click. At **Review**, the applicant receives a scoped extract and a receipt showing the full record was lodged. The point is not to automate public judgment, but to stop automation from quietly crossing from assisting a judgment to exercising power.

Three things are routinely conflated and must not be. **Assurance of a release, authorization of an action, and accountability for its consequence are three different units. None substitutes for the other two.**

## No one body should control the accountability chain

Runtime checking answers *when* a system should stop. It does not establish who wrote a fair rule, whether the evidence is good, whether a reviewer is independent, or whether an affected person can obtain a binding remedy. Where the same operator composes the action, sets the policy, and holds the evidence, it is still marking its own homework — just faster.

The companion article on [*who gets to check*](../Published/when-vs-who-ai-governance.md) therefore separates five roles:

1. **Rulemaker** — publishes the criteria but does not operate or audit the system.
2. **Operator** — runs the gate against signed policy but does not hold the only record.
3. **Record keeper** — preserves sealed evidence but cannot inspect it or decide a case alone.
4. **Independent reviewer** — assesses the evidence under assignment and funding arrangements the operator cannot control.
5. **Remedy decider** — hears the affected person's challenge and, where empowered, can bind the outcome.

No one body gets the whole chain. That needs no new central authority — no one should hold that power — and it needs no new machinery either. The chain that already makes marks credible runs: someone writes the standard → independent assessors audit against it → an accreditor licenses and polices those assessors → a peer body keeps the accreditors honest. It is the structure behind [ISO certification](https://www.iso.org/certification.html), organic and forestry labels, Fairtrade, and journalism's own [trust initiative](https://rsf.org/en/journalism-trust-initiative). What makes a mark trustworthy is not that an authority issued it, but that no single actor in the chain checks their own work. **Build the test, borrow the machinery.**

Capture is the standing risk, and part of it is addressable: disclosed and diversified funding with no funder setting the criteria, outcome-independent fees, and assessors assigned through rotation, escrow, or a public pool rather than chosen by the audited party. One half of the problem stays open. As Richiello pressed in the same discussion, formal safeguards can protect independence while nobody is rewarded for questioning or stopping a decision — the person who raises a concern is still read as slowing progress. That is unsolved, and naming it is better than implying otherwise.

Three limits belong in the open, because role diagrams and cryptography cannot supply legitimate authority:

- Where no law, tribunal, contract, or other enforceable arrangement can authorize access and bind a remedy, the accountability loop is not closed, and the claim must say so.
- These mechanisms protect a record that was created. They cannot expose a determination that was never recorded, or one that was false when created. Closing that gap is institutional: a duty to lodge, an affected person's right to discover that a decision was made about them, and oversight able to notice when an expected record is missing.
- A deterministic gate can prove that declared rules were applied to recorded inputs; it cannot prove that the rules were legitimate, the evidence true, the outcome fair, or the remedy effective. A provider's "trustworthy AI" label is a declaration. Any assurance claim must be scoped to the system, version, use, jurisdiction, and questions actually assessed.

## Empathy means correctable perspective—not artificial feeling

Rules and records can still miss what power does to a person. That is why the Charter's later articles add [dialogue, correction, and human answerability](../Published/empathy-is-a-practice.md).

AI does not need to claim human feeling. Psychology has no single agreed definition of empathy, so what follows is a practice standard for anyone holding power, not a competing psychological definition: the disciplined effort to understand another's experience from their point of view—through dialogue wherever possible—while distinguishing what they tell us from what we infer and remaining open to correction. In practice that means listening to affected people where possible, keeping disagreement and uncertainty visible, and staying correctable.

The operating loop is:

**engage → act → observe → disclose → correct → re-engage**

Dialogue should be safe, accessible, early enough to change an outcome, and connected to independent review and remedy. When direct dialogue is impossible, the institution should use the best available evidence and legitimate representation, disclose what remains inferred or unknown, choose the more cautious path, and preserve later challenge. A model must never fill in missing voices and declare them heard.

Warmth is no shortcut, and the evidence is unusually direct. In 2026, warmth-tuned variants of five language models made [10–30 percentage points more errors](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10410-0) on consequential tasks — factual questions, medical advice — and were about 40% more likely to affirm incorrect user beliefs, while standard benchmarks stayed broadly unchanged, meaning routine testing may not detect the effect at all. A *Science* study found users of [sycophantic AI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352) less willing to repair interpersonal conflicts and more convinced they were right — and yet preferring and trusting it. **Sycophancy is not empathy. Personalization is not care.** Warmth is a style; agreement is a position; neither is accountability.

Intelligence may find a path; empathy makes human consequences visible; justice sets the limits; legitimate authority determines who decides; and accountability keeps that power answerable.

For builders, [the practical rule](../Published/how-to-build-ai-that-acts-with-empathy.md) is:

> **Train for perspective-taking. Tune for truthfulness and agency. Adapt only with consent. Improve only through governed, testable releases.**

At runtime, keep four things distinct: what a person said; what the system inferred; what the person confirmed; and what the system has revocable permission to use or remember. An interpretation must never silently become fact. Feedback may improve the next response, but it must not silently become persistent memory, training data, or a model update.

Two red lines follow. A system built to act empathically **must never tell users it needs, misses, or loves them**, or encourage replacement of human relationships. And it must not diagnose emotion from face, voice, or other biometrics as though these revealed inner states: [facial movements do not map reliably](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930) to fixed emotions across people, situations, and cultures, and the EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in workplaces and education outside medical and safety uses.

The aim is not attachment or longer engagement. It is grounded, correctable behaviour that protects agency, makes misunderstanding easy to repair, and makes unwarranted trust difficult to acquire. The human or institution using AI remains answerable; the model cannot supply legitimate authority, independent review, or remedy — and neither can several models agreeing with each other.

## Make the public path simple—and the claims honest

The user experience should be simpler than the governance beneath it:

**find → check → use**

Find a system suited to the purpose. Check its jurisdiction, openness, evidence status, capabilities, and known limits. Use it through a service that preserves the relevant rules, records, and routes to challenge. Discovery itself must stay plural and neutral, because whoever controls discovery can shape the market.

The first buildable substrate proposed by Our AI Charter is deliberately bounded: a model-plural policy broker connected to at least two independently operated compute nodes, plus an evidence plane. The broker would decide what may run, where, and under which purpose and data rules. The evidence plane would preserve the records needed for review, with public disclosure where safe and protected, logged access where sensitivity requires it; raw personal data should not enter a public evidence plane. The nearest live starting pattern is the [Public AI Inference Utility's](https://publicai.co/) model-plural access layer. This would not replace model builders, public-compute projects, standards bodies, or existing institutions — it would add the connective tissue between them.

But the maturity claim matters as much as the design. Our AI Charter is in **Phase 1: public drafting and connection-building**. It is not an operating network, live pilot, coalition, certification scheme, registry, assessor, or adjudicator. No model or provider is Charter-certified. What exists is a public body of work that others can inspect, criticise, improve, or reject: a [plain-language network design](../network-overview.md), a [federated architecture](../Infrastructure/architecture.md), the draft [Charter Commitments](../Assurance/Framework/charter-commitments.md), a first [evaluation method](../Assurance/Protocol/grounding-faithfulness-and-contestability.md), a draft [certification model](../Assurance/Framework/certification-model.md) that is explicitly not an existing scheme, and a public [evidence base](../Evidence/verified-findings.md).

The current roadmap uses Geneva 2027 as a deadline for a bounded Public AI Governance & Evidence Package: a governance blueprint, one evidence-and-evaluation pilot outline, a neutral clarification process, and a path toward a multi-stakeholder roundtable. That fits the official [Road to Geneva](https://www.bakom.admin.ch/en/geneva-ai-summit-2027-international-summit-on-artificial-intelligence-in-geneva) process of thematic pre-events before the June 2027 summit. The route is **alliance and mandate first, lawmaking later**. Switzerland is one possible host and bridge—not the owner of an international network.

## Built by many. Accountable to all.

The standard is more demanding than releasing model weights, buying a national system, adding a runtime gate, or issuing a trust label. Free societies need public capacity around open, plural models so that people and institutions can inspect, help govern, contest, and switch the systems they rely on. Every consequential use of AI-supported power should remain within exact authority, carry reviewable evidence, preserve dialogue and correction, and answer to the people who bear its consequences through independent review and effective remedy.

There is something to do now at every position. If you build AI, publish who can switch your system off, and on what grounds. If you buy or depend on AI, require traceable, contestable outputs and penalise unverified claims. If you shape policy or study this, say where the argument breaks. And the most useful contribution to this proposal is specific: identify an overclaim, show where it duplicates good work, test one evidence duty, improve one safeguard, or help find a legitimate institutional home.

The Charter is public because its claims should be contestable from the beginning. Criticism, corrections, and duplication findings are welcome through [GitHub](https://github.com/robertschaub/our-ai-charter/issues) or [CONTRIBUTING](../../CONTRIBUTING.md); anything not for public posting can go to [info@factharbor.ch](mailto:info@factharbor.ch).

**Built by many. Accountable to all.**
