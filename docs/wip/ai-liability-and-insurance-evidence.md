# AI liability and insurance: who can compel evidence

*The question.* When an AI system contributes to a harm, who can actually compel evidence about how it behaved? Ahead of any AI-specific statute, two mechanisms are answering that in practice: **product-liability law**, which now treats software as a product and penalises non-disclosure, and **insurance underwriting**, which prices what it can assess. Both bear directly on the Charter's argument that accountability requires evidence someone outside the operator can check — so it is worth knowing precisely what they require today, rather than assuming. This note is a citable evidence base, not a conclusion.

**Short answer (assessment).** Both mechanisms are moving, and neither yet reaches independent evidence. Liability law is the sharper of the two: from December 2026 an EU defendant who cannot disclose relevant evidence faces a presumption that its product was defective. Insurance is the softer: underwriters increasingly ask for *documented internal governance*, and nothing found shows a carrier or broker requiring an audit trail or a per-action record as a condition of cover. "Show us your governance" and "show us evidence someone else can check" are different asks, and only the first is currently being made.

## What the same sources say against the thesis

Recorded because they bound the claim, not as decoration.

- **The market is not broadly retrenching.** Aon, in the same article as finding 3: "AI is reshaping risk at speed, but the insurance market is not responding with broad exclusions or sharp retrenchment. Instead, 2026 is defined by clarification, targeted adjustments and a deeper focus on how AI modifies exposure within established lines. Capacity remains available, but underwriting scrutiny is rising." An exclusion wave read as an assurance-demand signal is the weaker reading; *rising scrutiny inside available capacity* is the stronger one.
- **An exclusion is a substitute for assurance, not a demand for it.** Where a carrier can simply decline the exposure, evidence has no price. Evidence only becomes valuable where the insurer wants to write the risk — which is why findings 3–5 matter more to this thesis than findings 14–15. [assessment]
- **The Lloyd's market is asking for less regulation, not more.** Finding 7 is a published request that regulators enforce existing law rather than introduce new rules. Whatever is happening here, it is not the market calling for a new mandated obligation. [assessment]
- **Underwriters currently believe insureds are managing AI well** (finding 2). Any account premised on the market feeling unsafe would contradict what the market has published about itself. What the LMA does say is that this confidence rests on no collected evidence — a different and narrower point. [assessment]

## What did not hold up

- **No carrier or broker was found to require an audit trail or pre-execution evidence as a condition of coverage or pricing.** Scope of the search: published product pages of Munich Re (aiSure), Armilla and Mosaic; Lloyd's Market Association AI publications and its model-wordings material; Aon and WTW public AI publications; Swiss Re Institute and SVV publications; FINMA supervisory communications; and insurance trade press to 2026-08-06. Not searched, and where a requirement would most plausibly live: actual policy wordings, broker submission packets, and carrier application questionnaires — none of which are public. The absence is a search result, not a finding of fact.
- **Circulating quantified claims about AI-governance premium credits do not survive checking.** Specific figures (e.g. "premium reductions of 20% to 50%", "80% of companies using AI-powered defences receive premium credits", "AI Security Riders requiring proof of red-teaming") appear only on marketing and search-optimised sites with no traceable source. Do not cite them. A second-hand claim that firms without documented AI governance "face coverage denials at renewal", attributed to Willis research, could not be traced to a WTW publication and is treated as **unverified**.
- **Zurich Insurance's position could not be established.** Zurich appears in the S&P-derived list of groups filing to adopt or delay AI exclusions (finding 15); a separate trade report, not retrievable at source, was summarised as having it state no such plans for standard liability products. Neither reading is confirmed here, so no position is attributed — see open questions.

## Two limits on reading anything into this

- **FINMA 08/2024 is not an AI assurance scheme.** It is supervisory expectation for financial-market institutions, and it records that Switzerland has no AI-specific legislation ("Bislang existiert in der Schweiz keine KI-spezifische Gesetzgebung"). What findings 11–12 support is narrower and still useful: a Swiss supervisor has looked at AI traceability and independent review and published what it found lacking.
- **No relationship exists with any party named in this note.** Every finding is drawn from public material. None of it implies contact, interest, or endorsement in either direction.

## Open questions

- Does any carrier or broker in fact require an audit trail, decision record, or pre-execution evidence to bind or price cover? The public record cannot answer this; a single conversation with an underwriter or broker could.
- Have liability underwriters priced the Art. 10(2)(a) disclosure presumption ahead of the 9 December 2026 transposition, and does any wording or questionnaire yet reference evidence disclosability?
- Which Swiss transposition path applies? Switzerland is outside the directive, so the exposure route for Swiss suppliers is products placed on the EU market — the scope of that indirect effect was not researched here.
- Does the LMA's model-wordings work define "AI" or "AI system" in market wordings, and does any wording condition cover on demonstrable governance? The survey page references a related LMA Chief Underwriting Officer commission that was not retrieved in full.
- What is Zurich Insurance's actual published position on generative-AI exclusions?
- Is the Armilla/Munich Re relationship on the MKIII case a value chain — independent assessor → warranty → reinsurance capacity — or two separate arrangements? Both pages describe the same model; neither describes the other's role.
- Both affirmative-cover products rest on the insurer's or coverholder's *own* assessment of the model it then carries risk on. Whether any separation between assessor and risk-carrier exists elsewhere in this market could not be established from public sources.
